A star is a massive, luminous sphere of hot plasma held together by its own gravity. It is powered by nuclear fusion in its core, which generates an immense amount of light and heat. Composition • Hydrogen and Helium: Stars are made almost entirely of gas. They consist of roughly 75% hydrogen and 25% helium, with tiny traces of heavier elements like carbon, nitrogen, and iron. • Plasma: Because the temperatures are so extreme, the atoms are stripped of their electrons, turning the gas into a superheated, electrically charged state of matter called plasma. How Stars Form • Stellar Nurseries: Stars are born deep inside cold, dense clouds of interstellar gas and dust called nebulae. • Gravitational Collapse: Over millions of years, gravity pulls this material closer together. As the cloud condenses, it breaks into smaller clumps, and the core heats up to form a protostar. • Nuclear Ignition: Once the core temperature reaches roughly 15 million degrees Celsius, nuclear fusion begins. Hydrogen atoms smash together to form helium, releasing a massive burst of energy that causes the star to light up. Colors and Temperatures A star's color is a direct indicator of how hot it burns: • Blue Stars: These are the hottest stars in the universe, with surface temperatures often exceeding 25,000 °C. • Yellow Stars: These are medium-temperature stars. Our Sun is a yellow dwarf star, with a surface temperature of about 5,500 °C. • Red Stars: These are the coolest stars, with surface temperatures below 3,500 °C. The Life Cycle of Stars A star's lifespan is entirely determined by its mass. Ironically, larger stars burn through their fuel much faster than smaller ones: • Main Sequence: This is the long, stable phase of a star life where it actively fuses hydrogen. • Average Stars (like the Sun): When they run out of fuel, they expand into Red Giants, puff out their outer layers, and leave behind a cooling, dense core called a White Dwarf. • Massive Stars: These stars expand into Supergiants. When their fuel is spent, their cores collapse rapidly, triggering a massive cosmic explosion known as a Supernova. The remaining core becomes either a highly compressed Neutron Star or a Black Hole.
An asteroid is a rocky , airless remnant left over from the early formation of our solar system about 4.6 billion years ago.
